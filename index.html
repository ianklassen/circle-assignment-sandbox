Circle Assignment Sandbox

Life360 sells to households and experiments on people. Randomize the wrong unit and you either split families across two different experiences, or you buy a trustworthy readout with weeks you do not have. This sizes both costs before the test goes out.

The problem it solves

The billable unit is the Circle, so a paywall or a price is visible to the whole household. Assign at the user level and a parent sees one offer while their teenager sees another, which breaks the product and contaminates the readout.

Assign at the Circle level and the readout is clean, but you now have fewer independent units and their sizes vary, so the same test takes longer. Most teams discover that in week six.

What you get out of it

Three designs sized side by side: how many households get split, how long each takes to read, and how much time you save by adjusting for what those households were already doing before the test.

Then an independent validity gate rules on the design you picked. It does not care how fast the design is. The fastest design and the trustworthy one are rarely the same design, and the gate is what stops you shipping the fast one.

What this assumes Sizing an experiment always fixes a set of choices. These are the ones fixed here, stated rather than buried.

What is being measured

  • A yes or no outcome per person, such as converted or did not, rather than a continuous metric like revenue or days retained.
  • The outcome is attributed to each person, then aggregated. Circle members each count as an observation.
  • One primary metric. Guardrails are not sized here.

How the test is run

  • Two arms, split evenly. Control and one variant.
  • Fixed horizon. Read once at the end. Checking early without a sequential method inflates false positives, and none of these runtimes account for that.
  • 5% significance, two sided, and 80% power.
  • One experiment at a time on this surface, with no overlap from another live test.

Who is counted

  • Every Circle entering the experiment is actually exposed to the change. Real eligibility filters dilute this and lengthen the run.
  • Weekly traffic holds steady. No seasonality, no ramp, no marketing spike.
  • Household size and Circle overlap distributions are illustrative, not Life360 data.

The assumption that matters most: the outcome is counted per person. If what you actually measure is whether a household subscribes, then each Circle is a single observation, Circle size stops adding statistical power, and the clustering penalty disappears because there is nothing left to cluster. That is a different calculation from the one on this page, and knowing which of the two you are in is the first question to settle before sizing anything.

Method

  • Design effect for unequal cluster sizes: 1 + ((CV² + 1) · m̄ − 1) · ICC, where m̄ is mean Circle size and CV its coefficient of variation. Equal size clusters reduce this to the familiar 1 + (m̄ − 1) · ICC.
  • Sample size per arm, two proportion test at 5% two sided and 80% power: 2(zα/2 + zβ)² · p̄(1−p̄) / δ², then multiplied by the design effect.
  • Adjusting for past behaviour, the technique usually called CUPED, scales required sample by 1 − ρ², where ρ is the correlation between a pre period covariate and the metric. It changes only the analysis, never who is assigned to what, which is why that column differs from plain Circle assignment on runtime alone.
  • Probability a Circle of size m is split by person level assignment: 1 − 0.5m−1. Same form for a person in m Circles receiving conflicting assignments.
  • Household size and overlap distributions are illustrative, not Life360 data. The structure is what transfers. Replace the distributions with real ones and every number here sharpens.

Why a separate gate

  • A sizing tool answers how long. It cannot answer whether the answer will mean anything, because those pull in opposite directions: every shortcut that buys weeks costs validity. Keeping the two in separate layers is the only way the second one survives a deadline.
